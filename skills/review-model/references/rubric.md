# Money Model Review Rubric

## Provenance

Adapted from Greg Faxon's ten lessons in his [$100M Money Models summary](https://www.gregfaxon.com/blog/100m-money-models-summary). Criterion tests, the viability gate, grades, and ranking rules are reviewer adaptations. Third-party book and research claims remain unverified.

The compact table indexes the criteria; the complete Before → Move → After/Result → Transfer trajectories follow below and are required evidence for every review.

Use this reference for every review. Grade each criterion:

- `0 unknown`: evidence is missing or contradictory.
- `1 absent/fragile`: the capability is absent or fails in ordinary use.
- `2 partial`: it exists but is inconsistent, unproven, or constrained.
- `3 effective`: repeated evidence shows it works without unacceptable harm.

Confidence is separate: `low | medium | high`. A high-confidence grade of 1 is
more actionable than a low-confidence grade of 1.

## Ranking Rule

Prefer an intervention when it is close to the stated objective, supported by
current evidence, valuable to the customer, economically meaningful, feasible
within capacity, and testable without masking other variables. Reject moves
that manufacture complexity, depend on deceptive anchoring, worsen delivery,
or merely shift cash timing while harming retention or trust.


## Application Trajectories


These are structured paraphrases of the source video. Use them as analogies,
not prescriptions. For every criterion, compare the current business with the
trajectory and record `adapt | reject | investigate` plus the reason.

## 1. From one sale to a money-model sequence

- **Before:** A business thinks mainly about selling one core product.
- **Move:** Reframe the customer journey as attraction → upsell → downsell → continuity, where each step solves the next real problem.
- **After/result:** The business can recover acquisition cost earlier and keep serving customers after the first purchase.
- **Source illustration:** A low advertised rental-car rate expands through vehicle, time, insurance, and fuel choices; some additions are genuinely convenient.
- **Transfer test:** Does each proposed next offer create customer value, or merely extract more money?

## 2. From a large first commitment to a Strategy Sprint

- **Before:** Greg's qualified prospects faced an immediate roughly $30,000 annual coaching decision.
- **Attempt that failed:** A giveaway for a single coaching session did not work; a single session was not representative of his real offer and received weak design effort.
- **Move:** Productize the first month as a $2,500 Strategy Sprint: diagnose the bottleneck, refine the offer, create lead strategy, and produce a 90-day plan.
- **After/result:** Six clients bought the lower-risk start, producing a reported $14,500; the intake and short window also improved preparation and urgency.
- **Transfer test:** Can the entry offer deliver standalone value while honestly revealing mutual fit for the core engagement?

## 3. From completed diagnostic to prescribed implementation

- **Before:** A client finishes the sprint with clarity and a plan, but still faces implementation, accountability, and adjustment problems.
- **Move:** Convert the findings into a personalized strategy brief and hold a third call to prescribe the next 90 days or year.
- **After/result:** The annual coaching offer becomes the logical response to a newly evidenced problem, not a generic upsell.
- **Transfer test:** Is the next offer justified by delivery evidence from the first, or was it predetermined regardless of need?

## 4. From an unframed price to a credible anchor

- **Before:** A $2,500 sprint appears as an isolated price with little context.
- **Move:** Explain the credible full-year journey first (about $30,000), pause for the reaction, then offer the sprint as a smaller starting point.
- **After/result:** The entry offer is understood as lower-risk relative to the complete solution; the premium option remains genuinely purchasable.
- **Transfer test:** Is the anchor a real, valuable scope some buyers should choose, rather than an inflated decoy?

## 5. From repurchasing to rollover credit

- **Before:** Moving from the sprint to annual coaching could feel like starting and paying again.
- **Move:** Count the $2,500 sprint as month one, leaving $27,500 for the next eleven months or a lower upfront remainder.
- **After/result:** Greg reports that two sprint clients enrolled for the full year upfront, producing about $50,000.
- **Source analogy:** Pilot offered former Bench customers credit during a forced provider transition.
- **Transfer test:** Does prior spend/work genuinely contribute to the expanded solution, and are the economics explicit?

## 6. From default card fees to a payment choice

- **Before:** Annual card payments would create a large, avoidable processing cost.
- **Move:** Accept cards for the smaller sprint, then disclose the card fee and waive it for ACH on the larger purchase.
- **After/result:** Greg reports roughly $1,445 saved across three clients while preserving a convenient option.
- **Transfer test:** Are costs and alternatives transparent, lawful, and appropriate to buyer convenience and collection risk?

## 7. From product discounting to sequential payment downsells

- **Before:** A buyer wants the full solution but cannot accept the initial payment timing.
- **Move:** Preserve scope and offer payment alternatives one at a time: pay in full, financing/layaway, half now and later, three payments, then monthly.
- **After/result:** Greg reports two clients and about $31,000 through payment-plan downsells; the source argues for rewarding upfront payment and watching cannibalization/churn.
- **Transfer test:** Is the obstacle timing or affordability, and can a plan solve it without hiding cost or creating collection risk?

## 8. From “no” to a smaller honest scope

- **Before:** Some clients did not want the full coaching version, though they still valued strategic perspective.
- **Move:** Remove features instead of merely cutting price: quarterly calls, no between-call support, metrics review, and priority setting.
- **After/result:** A quarterly advisory offer at $1,500 per quarter or $6,000 upfront served a narrower need; three clients reportedly produced $18,000.
- **Transfer test:** Which exact features can be removed while the smaller offer still produces a coherent standalone outcome?

## 9. From monthly recurrence to prepaid continuity

- **Before:** Recurring work creates future revenue but limited immediate cash.
- **Move:** Offer fair reasons to commit earlier: annual discount, continuity bonus, or waived setup fee with clear cancellation terms.
- **After/result:** Two of Greg's first three quarterly-planning clients chose $6,000 upfront even without a discount, possibly because of year-end timing; he treats that explanation as a hypothesis to retest mid-year.
- **Transfer test:** Is prepayment valuable and voluntary, and is the observed reason for uptake proven rather than assumed?

## 10. From product sprawl to multiple offers around one core

- **Before:** Multiple products and experiments fragmented focus; Greg found one-to-one coaching produced about 80% of profit.
- **Move:** Simplify around the proven core while varying the way customers enter, expand, pay, reduce scope, and continue.
- **After/result:** Strategy Sprint, annual coaching, payment plans, and quarterly advisory became different commercial paths around the same expertise.
- **Transfer test:** Does the new mechanism strengthen the proven core, or create a second delivery business with weak evidence?
